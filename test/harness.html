Reading on a screen is harder than it looks

By A. Writer · 8 min read

When text is surrounded by banners, sidebars, autoplaying video and a dozen other things competing for attention, the eye has to work harder to stay on the line it is reading. For most people this is a minor annoyance. For readers with ADHD or dyslexia it can be the difference between finishing a page and giving up.

The fix does not have to be clever. In special education, a simple reading ruler — a strip of card with a slot cut out — has been used for decades. It does one thing: it hides everything except the line you are on.

The problem

Digital pages rarely offer that kind of focus. A typical article page loads with a sticky header, a newsletter modal, a cookie banner, a chat widget and a floating video player. Each one is designed to pull the eye away at the exact moment the reader is trying to concentrate.

The result is a reading experience that is measurably worse than paper: more regressions, more re-reading, and a lower chance of finishing. The problem is not that people cannot read. It is that the page keeps interrupting.

“A reading ruler is not a feature. It is an accommodation — the digital equivalent of turning down the volume in a noisy room.”

The interesting thing about this problem is how cheap it is to solve. A single overlay, one band of transparency, and a shadow that dims everything outside it. No servers, no accounts, no data collection. The entire mechanism fits in a few dozen lines of CSS.

And yet it is hard to find a tool that does exactly this, on any page, without asking for permission to read and change all of your data on all websites, and without turning the reading experience into a subscription.

How it works

You drag a band to the height where your eyes naturally rest. Everything above and below is dimmed. When you scroll, the band stays put and the text moves through it, like a physical ruler held against the page.

If the page is noisy, a second switch stops animations and lets you hide the elements that keep stealing focus. Settings are remembered per website, because reading a news site and reading a dashboard are two different jobs.

Results

The point is not to make reading faster. It is to make reading finishable. A quieter page, a single line of attention, and a tool that gets out of the way — that is the whole product.
